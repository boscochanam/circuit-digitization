\begin{abstract}
Recovering a netlist from a hand-drawn schematic requires deciding which wire strokes connect which component terminals. We present a deterministic pipeline for this structural connectivity task. An occlusion-first extractor masks components before binarization and returns wire segments with explicit endpoints. A typed endpoint graph links endpoints, pins and wire bodies under scale-relative tolerances, and a degree-budget completion step reconnects floating pins by bounded assignment. We evaluate on two real-image connectivity benchmarks from CGHD-1152: 31 images with human-verified nets, and 164 held-out photographs from 24 of the corpus's 25 drafters whose reference nets we derive from the dataset's own stroke and symbol annotations. With annotated component boxes, the join reaches component-pair micro-F1 0.890 on the human-verified set, on which its configuration was selected, and 0.711 on the held-out photographs. On both benchmarks it outperforms every deterministic baseline, including connected-component tracing and Hough line fitting, in paired tests with Holm correction. With annotated wires, its score stays within 0.005 of the native value when the images are rescaled from 0.5$\times$ to 3$\times$. Replacing the annotated boxes with a trained component detector gives micro-F1 0.627, and most of that loss comes from missed components. The wire extractor reaches F1 0.973 on 134 annotated images; on the held-out photographs, wire extraction is the main source of error. A vision-language model given the same component boxes reaches micro-F1 0.923 on the human-verified set: the pooled difference from our join is not significant, and per-image scores favor the model. We release the code, both benchmarks and the evaluation scripts.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

A circuit schematic encodes component types, component values and the connections between components in a drawing made for people. A netlist records the same connections in a form that software can check, simulate and search. Converting hand-drawn schematics into netlists would let course material, lab notebooks and legacy designs enter these tools without manual re-entry. This paper addresses the structural part of that conversion: given the components in an image, decide which component terminals share an electrical node. We call this step \emph{joining}.

Joining is hard for three reasons. First, wire endpoints extracted from binarized images are noisy: the same circuit drawn by different drafters yields endpoints at different offsets from the pins, and strokes stop short of or overshoot their targets. Second, component symbols overlap the wire endpoints that attach to them, so an endpoint near a component may belong to one of its pins or merely pass by. Third, T-junctions, collinear fragments and wire crossings all look alike to a proximity rule.

Most image-to-netlist systems erase the components and read nets from the connected components or contours of the remaining wire pixels~\cite{sina2026,peker2026}. A single stroke gap then splits a net, and a single touching stroke merges two. Other systems pass the image to a large language model (LLM) and read connectivity from its answer~\cite{masalachai2024}. We keep an explicit representation of every wire endpoint and decide each connection with geometric rules that can be inspected. This paper makes four contributions:
\begin{enumerate}
\item An \emph{occlusion-first wire extractor} that fills each component region with its local median color before Sauvola binarization and returns line segments with PCA-estimated endpoints. It reaches wire-detection F1 0.973 on 134 CGHD-1152 images (0.976 for the best of 36 variants).
\item A typed \emph{endpoint-graph join} with five edge rules, component-first pin assignment and scale-relative tolerances, followed by \emph{degree-budget completion}, which reconnects floating pins through a bounded-capacity assignment with a shared-component guard.
\item Two real-image connectivity benchmarks: 31 CGHD-1152 images with human-verified nets, and a 164-image held-out benchmark covering 24 of the 25 CGHD drafters whose reference nets are derived from the CGHD authors' own stroke-segmentation maps and symbol polygons. The derived reference agrees with the human nets on 17 overlapping images at micro-F1 0.939.
\item An evaluation protocol with paired significance tests under Holm correction, complexity and component-size strata, controlled image rescaling, end-to-end runs with a trained component detector, and a vision-language model (VLM) reference that receives the same component boxes as our join.
\end{enumerate}
The output is a structural netlist; recognizing component values and device models is outside the scope of this work.

Section~\ref{sec:related} reviews related work, Section~\ref{sec:method} describes the method and Section~\ref{sec:setup} the experimental setup. Section~\ref{sec:results} reports results, and Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses them together with the scope and limitations of the study.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

Several systems convert schematic images into machine-readable netlists. Kelly and Cole~\cite{kelly2024digitizing} presented a pattern-recognition workflow for printed schematics with an 86.4\% success rate, combining optical character recognition (OCR) for component values with network-graph construction. SINA~\cite{sina2026} pairs a YOLOv11 detector with connected-component labeling (CCL) and OCR, and uses a VLM only for reference-designator assignment, reporting 96.47\% netlist-generation accuracy, a 2.72$\times$ improvement over the prior state of the art. SINA derives nets from CCL proximity rather than from the VLM. Masala-CHAI~\cite{masalachai2024} uses large language models to generate SPICE descriptions of analog schematics at scale. The concurrent work of Peker et al.~\cite{peker2026} builds an automated image-to-SPICE pipeline for printed and hand-drawn transistor-level circuits. It pairs YOLOv8/11 detection with a contour-based node-detection module, in which components are erased and nets are read from the contours of the residual wire pixels, and it validates netlists by LTspice simulation against a reference. On an in-house seven-topology MOSFET dataset it reports 93.33\% (printed) and 85.33\% (hand-drawn) whole-netlist success. Its node detection belongs to the connected-component family that we evaluate as a baseline on identical wire inputs (Section~\ref{sec:real_eval}); its dataset and metric differ from ours, so the headline numbers are not directly comparable. Bayer et al.~\cite{instanceseg2023} segment components and wires for graph extraction on the CGHD lineage. For hand-drawn inputs, object-detection pipelines~\cite{handdrawn2021} and the JUHCCR-v1 database~\cite{juhccr2025} target component recognition on freehand sketches. Most of these pipelines build on general-purpose detectors such as the You Only Look Once (YOLO) family~\cite{redmon2016yolo,jocher2023yolo} and OCR engines such as Tesseract~\cite{smith2007tesseract}, and target simulator input formats such as SPICE~\cite{nagel1973spice}. SPICE-level modeling also supports hardware work in neighboring domains, including memristor-based neuromorphic circuits that realize brain-inspired learning~\cite{gao2026memristor} and memristive decision-making circuits for autonomous navigation~\cite{gao2026memristive}. We focus on the joining stage and evaluate it with both annotated and detected components.

Progress depends on annotated corpora. The CGHD dataset~\cite{bayer2021cghd} provides component- and wire-level annotations for handwritten schematics. In the analog/mixed-signal (AMS) domain, AMSnet~2.0~\cite{amsnet2025} contributes a large database built with AI-assisted segmentation for net detection, and AMSBench~\cite{amsbench2025} evaluates multimodal LLMs across AMS circuit tasks. DiagramNet~\cite{diagramnet2026} introduces a framework and dataset for non-standard system-level diagrams, including a connection-recognition task scored by F1. To our knowledge, no public benchmark reports net-level connectivity on hand-drawn circuit images; Peker et al.~\cite{peker2026} report whole-netlist success on an in-house hand-drawn set.

General-purpose VLMs and LLMs have been applied broadly to electronic design~\cite{llm4eda2024} and to circuit understanding. Kulkarni et al.~\cite{kulkarni2025enhancing} proposed Near Sight Correction with VLMs, reporting 87.4\% accuracy on circuit visual question answering and 0.87 F1 on connection-matrix prediction. On the DiagramNet connection task~\cite{diagramnet2026}, general models score low F1 (GPT-5 0.029, Claude-Sonnet-4 0.265, Gemini-2.5-Pro 0.008), whereas a task-tuned 3B model reaches 0.735. AMSBench~\cite{amsbench2025} reports a similar gap for AMS connectivity. These results vary with task and input, which motivates measuring VLM connectivity directly on our benchmark (Section~\ref{sec:vlm_results}).

Classical wire extraction relies on edge detection, morphological operations and the Hough transform~\cite{duda1972hough}, usually after adaptive binarization such as Sauvola thresholding~\cite{sauvola2000} and connected-component labeling~\cite{he2017ccl}. The legacy join in our pipeline uses radius-based proximity: each wire endpoint takes every component pin within a fixed threshold (30\,px), and connections are merged transitively with union-find~\cite{tarjan1975union}. When several components fall within the threshold, this rule assigns both pins of a two-terminal part to the same net. We model joining instead as a typed endpoint graph that uses component geometry, and we resolve residual ambiguity with degree-budget completion, a bounded $b$-matching formulation~\cite{kuhn1955,schrijver2003combinatorial} that caps the number of recovery connections per pin. Graph neural networks for netlist reverse engineering~\cite{alrahis2021gnn} learn representations over already-extracted gate-level graphs; our graph is geometric, and its edges encode spatial relations between endpoints and pins. Netlistify~\cite{netlistify2025} predicts terminal connectivity with a Transformer and reports a 12.4\% F1 gain over AMSnet on printed AMS schematics; such learned models need connectivity training data, whereas our join needs none. Open hand-drawn efforts such as CircuitNet~\cite{circuitnet} combine traditional image processing with a classifier and a heuristic netlist generator.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Pipeline Overview}

Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline} summarizes the six stages: (1)~component detection with an oriented-bounding-box (OBB) YOLO model; (2)~component occlusion, which fills each component region, enlarged by 15\% of its box size (at least 5\,px), with the median color of its surroundings; (3)~Sauvola binarization ($k=0.285$, window 67\,px) inside the union of component boxes plus 10\,px padding; (4)~connected-component labeling after a $3\times3$ elliptical closing, discarding components under 28\,px; (5)~wire-segment and endpoint estimation by principal component analysis (PCA) of each connected component, followed by overlap deduplication ($12^{\circ}$, 8\,px) and an anchor filter with a 16\,px endpoint distance; and (6)~joining with the endpoint graph and degree-budget completion. The output maps each component pin to an electrical node. Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline_examples} shows two circuits at each stage.

\subsection{Endpoint-Graph Join Model}

Given wire segments $\mathcal{W} = \{w_1, \ldots, w_m\}$ with endpoints $\text{ep}(w_i) = (a_i, b_i)$ and component pins $\mathcal{P} = \{p_1, \ldots, p_n\}$, we construct an undirected graph $G = (V, E)$ with $V = \text{eps}(\mathcal{W}) \cup \mathcal{P}$ and five edge types.

\textbf{Edge type 1 (wire body):} For each wire $w_i$, add edge $(a_i,b_i)$ so that both endpoints belong to the same net.

\textbf{Edge type 2 (endpoint--endpoint):} For endpoints $e_i,e_j$ on different wires with $\|e_i-e_j\|\leq\tau_{\text{join}}$, add an edge. This rule connects fragments, corners and junctions.

\textbf{Edge type 3 (endpoint--pin):} Each endpoint connects to at most one pin. The component-first assignment described below chooses the pin. If no component is assigned, the endpoint takes the pin within $d\leq\tau_{\text{pin}}$ with the smallest score, where the score is $d[1-\alpha\max(0,\cos\theta)]$ with $\alpha=0.35$ and $\theta$ the angle between the outward wire direction and the endpoint-to-pin vector (for $d\le10^{-6}$\,px the score is $d$). If neither step finds a pin, no edge is added.

\textbf{Edge type 4 (endpoint--wire-body):} For an endpoint $e$ and a different segment $w_j$, add an edge if the point-to-segment distance is at most $\tau_t$ and the normalized projection parameter lies in $[0.15,0.85]$. This rule captures T-junctions away from segment ends.

\textbf{Edge type 5 (pin--wire-body):} For a pin $p$ and a segment $w_j$, add an edge if the point-to-segment distance is at most $\tau_t$ and the projection parameter lies in $[0.05,0.95]$. This rule captures pins tapped onto a rail.

The tolerances are scale-relative with fixed pixel clamps: $\tau_{\text{pin}}=\min(60,\max(24,0.62s))$, $\tau_{\text{join}}=\min(28,\max(11,0.30s))$ and $\tau_t=\min(20,\max(8,0.20s))$, in pixels. Here $s$ is the median component-box diagonal of the image (the upper middle value for an even count), with a wire-length scale as fallback when component geometry is unavailable. The multipliers and clamps are set by hand. Because $s$ is one scalar per image, these three tolerances do not vary with component size inside a drawing.

\textbf{Component-first pin assignment:} For edge type 3, each endpoint is first assigned to a component. Its distance to a component is 0 inside the component's OBB and otherwise the distance to the nearest OBB edge (axis-aligned box distance when OBB vertices are unavailable). The endpoint is assigned to the nearest component within $\max(\tau_{\text{pin}}, 0.5\,\delta_c)$, where $\delta_c$ is that component's own box diagonal. Because this radius grows with each component's diagonal, endpoint-to-component binding follows the size of each component in addition to the image-level scale $s$. Within the assigned component, the endpoint takes the nearest pin. Figure~\ref{fig:endpoint_graph} contrasts the legacy radius join with the endpoint graph.

Nets are the connected components of $G$, accumulated with union-find and projected onto pins. The result maps each pin to a net identifier.

\subsection{Degree-Budget Completion}

After base joining, some pins lie in a net that contains only pins of their own component. Completion treats these \emph{floating} pins as candidates for recovery, excluding inert types (ground, supply markers, text, antennas, probes, junctions and terminals).

Completion keeps the base nets and proposes connections from each floating pin $p$ to pins $q$ of other components in other nets. A candidate's distance must not exceed the reach $r=\rho\tau_c$, where $\tau_c=\min(60,\max(24,0.62s))$\,px and $\rho=4$. For each candidate we look for a wire witness: a detected segment whose endpoints lie near $p$ and $q$ in either orientation. The witness cost is the smallest, over segments and orientations, of the larger endpoint-to-pin distance, bounded by $r$. Without a witness, the candidate cost is $\|p-q\|$.

The candidates form a rectangular assignment matrix with one row per floating pin. Each target pin supplies up to three slot columns, limited by its candidate demand, and each row has a private dummy column of cost $1.5r$ for opting out. Unavailable assignments cost $10^6$. The Hungarian algorithm~\cite{kuhn1955} (\texttt{scipy.optimize.linear\_sum\_assignment}) assigns every row to one column, with at most one row per column; the target slots implement bounded-capacity matching~\cite{schrijver2003combinatorial}.

Matches are applied in sequence. Dummy and unavailable assignments add no edge. A merge is skipped if both pins are already in the same net or if their nets already share a component; this guard acts on completion merges only. Completion edges have no corresponding wire segment, much like a manual pin merge in a schematic editor. Figure~\ref{fig:completion} illustrates the step.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\subsection{Datasets and Image Provenance}

All real images come from CGHD-1152~\cite{bayer2021cghd}, a public corpus of hand-drawn circuit images organized by drafter. We use four evaluation sets. The \emph{wire benchmark} has 134 images with human wire labels and annotated component boxes. The \emph{human-verified net benchmark} has 31 images from 10 drafters. The 34 annotated images were chosen by circuit content, not appearance: ten initial images spanning a range of complexity, and the rest sampled at even intervals of electrical-component count from the circuits whose parts all belong to the scored device types. Image background was not a selection criterion, but the selected copies have mostly plain backgrounds, partly because 16 of them are stroke maps (see below). Three annotated images were excluded before scoring (one unverified, two failing physical-sanity checks). Net proposals were bootstrapped by a conservative endpoint-graph join over the annotated wires and corrected by one annotator in a purpose-built verification tool. The 31 images contain 3--14 electrical components each (median 7); 15 have at most five and 12 have at least ten. The \emph{independent held-out benchmark} (164 images, 24 drafters) and the \emph{synthetic suite} (15 authored circuits) are described in Sections~\ref{sec:cghd_ref} and~\ref{sec:synthetic}.

The 134 wire-benchmark and 31 net-benchmark images are $704\times704$ copies from a Roboflow export of CGHD. The export resized each original without preserving its aspect ratio. Of the 31 net-benchmark images, 14 are also flipped or rotated relative to the CGHD file, and 16 (45 of the 134 wire-benchmark images) are CGHD's binary stroke-segmentation renderings instead of photographs. Wire and net labels were drawn on these copies. The held-out benchmark uses the original CGHD photographs.

\subsection{Connectivity Metric}

For each image we form the set of unordered pairs of electrical components that share at least one net, and compare predicted with reference pairs. The electrical subset contains resistors, capacitors, inductors, diodes, transistors, sources and integrated circuits; junctions, terminals, ground symbols and crossovers affect the score only through the connections they mediate. The metric ignores which terminal of a component carries a connection. Micro-F1 pools true positives (TP), false positives (FP) and false negatives (FN) over images, so a dense board contributes in proportion to its connections; it is our primary metric. Macro-F1, the mean of per-image F1, is reported alongside.

\subsection{Statistical Protocol}

All comparisons are paired over images. We report 95\% percentile confidence intervals (CIs) from an image-level bootstrap with $B=10{,}000$ resamples, and test each difference with three procedures: the bootstrap itself, a sign-flip permutation test that swaps the two methods' outputs within each image (exact when at most 22 images differ, otherwise 100{,}000 Monte Carlo draws), and a Wilcoxon signed-rank test on per-image F1. The first two test the micro-F1 difference; the Wilcoxon test compares images. Holm's correction is applied within each procedure across the comparisons of a table. We also count per-image wins, ties and losses (W/T/L). Unless stated otherwise (Section~\ref{sec:e2e}), every join receives annotated component boxes and the pipeline's own detected wires.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Synthetic Evaluation}
\label{sec:synthetic}

We authored 15 circuits with 3--6 components and 2--6 nets, including parallel resistors, series dividers, Wheatstone bridges, RC/RL circuits, diode loops and a 6-component ring. Each circuit has a known netlist, component values and a test voltage source. We inject five error types at four severity levels (L1--L4) on top of the clean level L0: Gaussian endpoint jitter ($\sigma$ from 3\,px at L1 to 14\,px at L4); cut-short wires, shortened inward by 4\,px at L1 to 22\,px at L4; wire drops with probability up to 0.20 at L4; wrong-pin snaps with probability from 0.02 at L2 to 0.10 at L4; and anchor displacement, which moves one endpoint by up to 30\,px at L1 and 80\,px at L4. Besides component-pair F1, we report simulation accuracy: the percentage of seeds for which every source current of the predicted netlist, simulated with the authored values, matches the clean reference within 5\%.

Several image-to-netlist systems erase component regions and read nets from the remaining wire pixels with connected components or contours~\cite{sina2026,peker2026}. On our detected wires, the best connected-component configuration reaches micro-F1 0.624 at precision 0.965: it rarely merges wrongly, but every stroke gap splits a net (recall 0.461). Both baselines use the best configuration of their sweep on these 31 images, which favors them.

We also tried to run five public image-to-netlist systems on the benchmark. The printed-schematic interpreter of Kelly and Cole~\cite{kelly2024digitizing} runs, but on hand-drawn scans it over-segments wires into spurious components and recovers almost no connectivity. Netlistify~\cite{netlistify2025} reproduces on its own printed AMS test data, but its released model lacks the diode, inductor and IC classes in our circuits, and its connectivity model is trained on printed thin-line drawings. Masala-CHAI~\cite{masalachai2024} delegates connectivity to a proprietary LLM behind a paid API, which our VLM reference (Section~\ref{sec:vlm_results}) covers. The code archive of SINA~\cite{sina2026} could not be extracted, and CircuitNet~\cite{circuitnet} ships notebooks without trained weights. The CCL and Hough baselines therefore stand in for these approaches on identical inputs.

On this benchmark, the extracted wires are as good as the annotated ones for joining. The join also scores alike on both image modalities: micro-F1 is 0.883 on the 15 photographs and 0.895 on the 16 stroke maps.

\subsection{Independent Held-Out Benchmark}
\label{sec:cghd_ref}

The 31-image benchmark has three weaknesses: it is small, its labels were bootstrapped from a graph join similar to ours, and our configuration was chosen on it. We therefore built a second benchmark whose labels come from the CGHD authors' own annotations and whose images were not used to develop or tune the join. The extractor configuration was selected earlier on the wire benchmark, whose resized copies include 25 of the held-out drawings; excluding those 25 changes none of the results below.

\textbf{Reference construction.} CGHD provides binary stroke-segmentation maps and symbol instance polygons for a subset of its images. Extending the dataset's own wire-generation tooling, we subtract the dilated symbol polygons from the stroke map and take the remaining connected components as conductors. A conductor connects every polygon it touches, except text; junction polygons merge the conductors they touch. At a crossover polygon, the arms are paired straight through by their local direction ($180^{\circ}\pm45^{\circ}$), and a crossover that cannot be paired is flagged as ambiguous. Composite outlines (transformers, optocouplers and relays that contain sub-parts) group their parts but take no contacts. Of 257 images, 179 pass all quality checks; the others have an ambiguous crossover (29), an isolated part (17) or an over-connected part (28).

\textbf{Validation.} On the 17 images shared with the human-verified benchmark, the reference agrees with the human nets at micro-F1 0.939 (P 0.993, R 0.891; exact on 11 of 17; 0.964 on the 15 clean images). We inspected every disagreement. Most are pairs the reference misses because of stroke gaps or ambiguous crossings, and seven follow from a convention: the human nets bridge switches. In five pairs, the reference was right and the human labels wrong. The reference mainly under-connects, so it understates the precision of every method. We fixed the quality filter and a junction-handling rule after inspecting these 17 overlaps.

\textbf{Protocol.} The held-out set contains the 164 clean images outside the 31, with 3874 reference pairs from 24 drafters (all except drafter\_0, which the detector's training also excluded). The input is the CGHD photograph with its aspect ratio preserved and its long side resized to 1024\,px. We chose this resolution on wire-level labels of the 52 wire-benchmark overlaps, before computing any join score. Unlike the 31-image benchmark, which kept only circuits whose parts all belong to the scored device types, the held-out set also contains switches, potentiometers, logic gates, speakers, thyristors and transformers (94 of the 164 images contain at least one); these parts take part in the topology but are not scored.

\subsubsection{Component-size variation within an image}
A single scale $s$ per image could fail when component sizes vary within a drawing. On the 31 images, the overall dispersion of component sizes is unrelated to F1 ($\rho=+0.18$, $p=0.33$). The ratio of the largest to the smallest electrical-component diagonal correlates with F1 at $\rho=-0.60$, but it also grows with component count ($\rho=0.62$); controlling for count, the partial correlation is $-0.41$ ($p=0.024$) for our join and $-0.34$ for the VLM. The size of the largest component relative to $s$ has partial correlation near zero with F1. The three images that combine an IC with small discrete parts score 0.854 against 0.898 on the other 28.

\subsubsection{Controlled rescaling}
We resampled all 31 images by factors from 0.35 to 3 and re-projected the annotated boxes and wires (Fig.~\ref{fig:rescale}). With annotated wires, our join stays within $\pm$0.005 of its native score from 0.5$\times$ to 3$\times$, with every CI including zero. It drops only at 0.35$\times$ ($-0.035$ [$-0.061$, $-0.005$]), where the lower pixel clamps bind for every image. With annotated wires, radius union-find with its fixed 30\,px radius falls from 0.690 at native scale to 0.429 at 2$\times$ and 0.153 at 3$\times$. With wires re-extracted from each rescaled image, the full pipeline scores 0.580, 0.690, 0.811, 0.890, 0.877, 0.866 and 0.866 at 0.35, 0.5, 0.75, 1, 1.5, 2 and 3$\times$. Upscaling costs at most 0.024 (CIs include zero). The loss under downscaling comes from wire extraction, whose Sauvola window, minimum component area and occlusion margins are set in pixels: at 0.35$\times$ the extractor finds 241 of 675 annotated segments. Completion recovers much of that loss; the scale-relative base alone scores 0.21 at 0.35$\times$.

To separate the contribution of the scale-relative tolerances, we ran the same algorithm with fixed pixel tolerances ($\tau_{\text{pin}}$, $\tau_{\text{join}}$, $\tau_t$ of 30, 14 and 10\,px, completion reach 120\,px). It stays within 0.016 of our join between 0.5$\times$ and 2$\times$. The scale-relative version wins only where the fixed values are most mismatched: by $+0.015$ with annotated wires at 0.5$\times$ and by $+0.033$ in the full pipeline at 3$\times$, both with CIs excluding zero. Most of the scale robustness therefore comes from component-relative endpoint assignment and completion; the scalar tolerances $\tau=k\cdot s$ contribute a smaller share. The clamps are tuned to native resolution: $k\cdot s$ falls outside a clamp for 39\% of images at native scale, and removing the clamps costs 0.023 in the full pipeline at native scale.

\subsubsection{Synthetic mixed-size circuits}
We redrew one component per synthetic circuit at 0.5, 2 or 3 times its size, keeping only geometrically clean variants, and applied the L1--L4 error model. Enlarging one component costs our join about 0.005 in mean F1, and it stays about 0.03 above the fixed-pixel variant. Shrinking one component costs 0.012. The failure case is a Wheatstone bridge with one 40$\times$15\,px arm: in all five variants, the scale-relative methods and radius union-find add one spurious pair even on clean wires, while the fixed-pixel graphs stay exact. A component much smaller than the median is thus the weak point of a median-derived tolerance.

\subsection{Crossovers}
\label{sec:crossovers}

Edge types 2, 4 and 5 do not consult crossover labels, so a missed crossover could merge two independent wires. The 31 images contain 13 annotated crossovers in eight images. With annotated boxes, recall on these eight images is 0.834, against 0.884 on the 23 without a crossover ($p=0.51$). In C242\_D1\_P1, suppressing the two endpoint--endpoint edges adjacent to crossings changes TP/FP/FN from 27/4/0 to 19/0/8: the edges that create the false pairs also carry true connections. Deleting the annotated crossover boxes before joining changes predictions on 6 of the 8 crossover images and raises micro-F1 by 0.010, so on annotated boxes the crossover labels do not help the join overall. In the end-to-end run, the detector finds all 13 crossovers and labels each as a crossover at every tested threshold, so none of the false pairs in Table~\ref{tab:e2e} comes from a missed or misclassified crossover; removing the detected crossovers lowers end-to-end micro-F1 by 0.019. These images were probably in the detector's training split, so this is not a held-out estimate of crossover recall, and thirteen instances are too few to test the 70.7\% validation recall.

\subsection{VLM Reference}
\label{sec:vlm_results}

We ran Claude Opus~4.8 as a reference system. It receives the annotated boxes of the electrical components, drawn on the image and numbered without class labels; our join receives the same boxes with their classes, and neither method receives annotated wires. The VLM was run on the human-verified benchmark only. The model returns JSON sets of component terminals that share an electrical node; each image is a separate call with no shared context. We score its output with the same component-pair metric.

The VLM reaches micro-F1 0.923 (P 0.970, R 0.880; macro-F1 0.949) and is exact on 21 of 31 images, against 11 for our join. On pooled pairs, our join trails by $-0.033$ [$-0.078$, $+0.008$], which is not significant (Holm-adjusted bootstrap $p=0.12$, permutation $p=0.15$; Table~\ref{tab:real_join}). Per image, the VLM is better: it wins 13 images, ties 11 and loses 7 (Wilcoxon $p=0.030$). Its errors follow the same complexity trend as ours (Table~\ref{tab:strata}). The two systems differ in what they require: the VLM needs a call to a proprietary API for every image and returns no geometric trace of its decisions, whereas our join runs locally and deterministically without connectivity training data, and every recovered connection maps to a wire segment or a completion edge.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The endpoint-graph join with completion is the strongest deterministic method on both real benchmarks, and its margins are significant on both, which differ in labels, images and drafters. The held-out benchmark addresses the three main weaknesses of the human-verified set: its labels come from the dataset authors' annotations and not from a join, it covers 24 of the 25 drafters, and its images were not used to develop the join. Completion accounts for most of the gain over the base graphs, and on the high-precision scale-relative base it adds recall while keeping precision above 0.9.

The experiments also locate the remaining errors. With annotated components on clean copies, extracted wires are as good as annotated ones. On difficult photographs, wire extraction limits accuracy; with detected components, missed detections do. Three changes follow from these results: an extractor whose pixel parameters follow the component scale, resampling inputs to a nominal component size, and a detector trained with full rotation augmentation. The VLM reference shows that a general model given component boxes can exceed a geometric join per image on this task. The geometric join remains useful where a local, deterministic and inspectable result is required.

\subsection{Scope and Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\textbf{Task and metric.} The pipeline recovers structural connectivity. It does not read component values or device models, so its SPICE export is illustrative and uses placeholder values; simulation of real scans is not evaluated. The component-pair metric ignores terminal identity: a perfect score does not certify pin assignments, the exact net partition or the absence of shorts between pins of one component. The completion guard blocks new same-component merges but does not repair merges made by the base graph. Table~\ref{tab:capabilities} lists the device groups: pin templates for transformers, optocouplers and other complex devices exist but are not validated, and the detector merges several original classes into one label.

\textbf{Data.} All images come from one corpus. The human-verified nets were bootstrapped from a conservative endpoint-graph join and corrected by a single annotator, which may leave a bias toward graph-based methods; on the held-out benchmark, with labels from the dataset's own annotations, our join again ranks first. The inspection of the 17 overlaps found five human-label errors (four in C242\_D1\_P1); we left the released human-verified labels unchanged. The derived reference is not human-verified, mainly misses connections, and its quality filter and a junction rule were fixed after inspecting the 17 overlapping images. The benchmark copies of the 31 images are resized, partly re-oriented, and half stroke maps, which makes them easier than raw photographs of the same drawings. The Hough and CCL baselines keep their 31-image settings on the held-out photographs.

\textbf{Scale.} The join tolerances derive from one median scale per image, and their clamps are tuned to native resolution. A component much smaller than the median can create a spurious pair, as in the synthetic Wheatstone case. The extractor's pixel-valued parameters limit accuracy on downscaled inputs. Rescaling tests changes in zoom, not in drawing style, and uses annotated components.

\textbf{Detector.} The detector was trained on CGHD images with an 85/15 random split, and no manifest of that split survives. The 31 benchmark drawings come from drafters eligible for training, and about 26 of them would be expected in the training split, so the end-to-end numbers are likely optimistic for detection. The detector excludes drafter\_0, whose drawing style differs from the rest of the corpus, and it is sensitive to orientation.

\textbf{Coverage.} The human-verified benchmark contains 13 annotated crossovers, and we did not evaluate dense multi-track buses or multi-layer crossings. The VLM reference uses one model and one prompt, and was run only on the human-verified benchmark. The synthetic error model is authored and not calibrated to measured detector errors.

\textbf{Expansion.} The derived reference makes a larger human-verified benchmark affordable: its nets can seed the verification tool, which logs a timestamp at every save so that per-image effort can be measured. We plan to sample new images stratified by component count, drafter, crossing and bus structure and capture conditions, have two annotators correct each image independently with adjudication of disagreements, and extend the evaluation to printed schematics and to corpora other than CGHD.

\section{Conclusion}

We presented a deterministic pipeline for structural netlist recovery from hand-drawn schematics, built on an occlusion-first wire extractor, a typed endpoint-graph join and degree-budget completion. With annotated components, the join reaches component-pair micro-F1 0.890 on 31 human-verified images and 0.711 on 164 held-out photographs with independently derived reference nets, and it beats every deterministic baseline on both with significant paired margins. Its accuracy holds from 0.5$\times$ to 3$\times$ image scale when wires are given, and it reaches 0.627 end to end with a trained detector. A VLM given the same components scores higher per image, at the cost of a proprietary call per image. Better extraction on difficult photographs and better component detection are the most direct routes to higher end-to-end accuracy.
